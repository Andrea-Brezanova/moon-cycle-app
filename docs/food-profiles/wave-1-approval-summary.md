## Proposed cycle-relevance wording (exact)

The section is built from up to three parts, in this order. Nothing else is allowed in it.

1. **Sourced nutrient fact (only where it applies).** One of:
   - Iron (serving gives 10% or more of the reference): "Iron needs are higher for women aged 19 to 50 (18 mg a day, against 8 mg for men), partly because of menstrual blood loss (NIH ODS), **and that need applies every day of the month**; this serving has about {x} mg."
   - Calcium (10% or more): "Calcium needs are 1,000 mg a day for women aged 19 to 50 (NIH ODS); this serving has about {x} mg."
   - Folate or vitamin C with plant iron: the existing NIH ODS sentences, unchanged.
2. **A plain statement that the cycle is not claimed (always):** "We found no reliable research linking {food} to a particular phase of the cycle."
3. Nothing about hormones, fertility, PMS, mood, energy, "balance" or "support".

Replaces the current closing line "No cycle-phase claim is made for {food}." (clearer: it says what was looked for and not found). **Heading (your preference): "Nutrition & your cycle".** It is used in the previews below only; the app still shows "Cycle relevance" until you approve publication, and the five already-published profiles are not changed.

## Representative complete profiles with the proposed wording

### Beef  (featured in the menstrual phase; editorial)

*Record:* USDA FDC 171793, "Beef, ground, 90% lean meat / 10% fat, patty, cooked, pan-broiled" (4/1/2019). *Serving:* 3 ounces cooked (USDA: 3 oz = 85 g). *Per serving:* 173 kcal; Vitamin B12 2.4 µg (99%), Zinc 5.4 mg (67%), Iron 2.4 mg (13%), Protein 21.4 g.

1. **What this food offers.** Cooked 90% lean ground beef is a dense source of vitamin B12 (about 99% of the reference in 3 ounces), zinc (about 67%) and iron (about 13%), with about 21 g of protein.
2. **A practical serving.** 3 ounces cooked (USDA: 3 oz = 85 g)
3. **How to eat it.** Brown for sauces, chilli and tacos, shape into burgers, or mix with grated vegetables or lentils to stretch it. Pair with vitamin C foods such as tomatoes or peppers.
4. **Nutrition & your cycle (proposed).** Iron needs are higher for women aged 19 to 50 (18 mg a day, against 8 mg for men), partly because of menstrual blood loss (NIH ODS), and that need applies every day of the month. Iron from meat is absorbed more readily than iron from plant foods, so beef is an efficient source; 3 ounces provide about 2.4 mg. We found no reliable research linking beef to a particular phase of the cycle.
   *Currently:* Iron needs are higher for women aged 19 to 50 (18 mg a day, against 8 mg for men), partly because of menstrual blood loss (NIH ODS). Iron from meat is absorbed more readily than iron from plant foods, so beef is an efficient source; 3 ounces provide about 2.4 mg. No phase-specific claim is made.
5. **Good to know.** Cook steaks, chops and roasts to 145 °F (62.8 °C) and let them rest for at least 3 minutes; cook ground meat to 160 °F (71.1 °C).
   *Sources:* USDA FSIS, Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart (read 2026-10-27)

### Kale  (featured in the menstrual phase; editorial)

*Record:* USDA FDC 169238, "Kale, cooked, boiled, drained, without salt" (4/1/2019). *Serving:* ½ cup cooked (USDA: 1 cup = 118 g). *Per serving:* 21 kcal; Vitamin C 11 mg (14%), Vitamin A 86 µg RAE (12%), Vitamin K 247 µg, Potassium 85 mg (3%).

1. **What this food offers.** Kale is a sturdy leafy green that is a good source of vitamin C and vitamin A, with plenty of vitamin K and fibre.
2. **A practical serving.** ½ cup cooked (USDA: 1 cup = 118 g)
3. **How to eat it.** Simmer it in soups and stews, sauté with garlic, or massage raw leaves with a little oil and lemon for a salad. Stems are tougher: slice them thinly or cook them longer.
4. **Nutrition & your cycle (proposed).** We found no reliable research linking kale to a particular phase of the cycle.
   *Currently:* No cycle-phase claim is made for kale.
5. **Good to know.** Very high vitamin K: keep intake consistent if taking warfarin
   *Sources:* NIH ODS Vitamin K (interaction with warfarin and other anticoagulants) (supports the existing caution)

### Almond  (featured in the ovulatory phase; editorial)

*Record:* USDA FDC 2346393, "Nuts, almonds, whole, raw" (10/28/2022). *Serving:* 30 g (USDA reference amount), a small handful. *Per serving:* 188 kcal; Magnesium 77 mg (25%), Iron 1.1 mg (6%), Zinc 0.9 mg (11%).

1. **What this food offers.** Almonds are a crunchy tree nut with plant protein, fibre, magnesium, iron and zinc. They are energy dense, so a small handful goes a long way.
2. **A practical serving.** 30 g (USDA reference amount), a small handful
3. **How to eat it.** Eat as a snack, chop over porridge or salads, or blend into nut butter. Soak and peel for a milder taste in sauces.
4. **Nutrition & your cycle (proposed).** We found no reliable research linking almond to a particular phase of the cycle.
   *Currently:* No cycle-phase claim is made for almond.
5. **Good to know.** Tree-nut allergen / Nuts are high in oxalate. If you have had calcium oxalate kidney stones, ask a health professional how many to eat.
   *Sources:* FDA, Food Allergies (the nine major food allergens; content current as of 03/11/2026; read 2026-10-28) (supports the existing caution); NIDDK, Eating, Diet & Nutrition for Kidney Stones (lists nuts and nut products among foods to reduce for calcium oxalate stones)

### Arugula (rocket)  (featured in the follicular phase; editorial)

*Record:* USDA FDC 169387, "Arugula, raw" (4/1/2019). *Serving:* 2 cups (USDA: 0.5 cup = 10 g). *Per serving:* 10 kcal; Folate 39 µg DFE (10%), Vitamin C 6 mg (8%), Vitamin K 44 µg.

1. **What this food offers.** Arugula (rocket) is a peppery salad leaf. Two cups give vitamin K, folate, calcium and some vitamin A for about 10 calories.
2. **A practical serving.** 2 cups (USDA: 0.5 cup = 10 g)
3. **How to eat it.** Toss it raw into salads, pile it onto pizza or pasta after cooking, or blend it into pesto. Its peppery bite softens when wilted.
4. **Nutrition & your cycle (proposed).** Folate is a nutrient some women of childbearing age do not get enough of (NIH ODS), and leafy greens such as arugula contribute some. We found no reliable research linking arugula (rocket) to a particular phase of the cycle.
   *Currently:* Folate is a nutrient some women of childbearing age do not get enough of (NIH ODS), and leafy greens such as arugula contribute some. No cycle-phase claim is made.
5. **Good to know.** Arugula (rocket) contains vitamin K. If you take warfarin or a similar blood thinner, keep your intake steady and tell your clinician.
   *Sources:* NIH ODS Vitamin K (interaction with warfarin and other anticoagulants)

### Sardine  (featured in the menstrual phase; editorial)

*Record:* USDA FDC 175139, "Fish, sardine, Atlantic, canned in oil, drained solids with bone" (4/1/2019). *Serving:* 1 small can, drained (USDA: 1 can, 3.75 oz = 92 g). *Per serving:* 191 kcal; Vitamin B12 8.2 µg (343%), Calcium 351 mg (35%), Vitamin D 4.4 µg (29%), Protein 22.6 g, Omega-3 (EPA + DHA) 0.9 g.

1. **What this food offers.** Canned sardines, eaten with their soft bones, are rich in calcium (about 35% of the reference in one small can), vitamin B12 and vitamin D, with about 0.9 g of omega-3 fats and 23 g of protein.
2. **A practical serving.** 1 small can, drained (USDA: 1 can, 3.75 oz = 92 g)
3. **How to eat it.** Mash on toast with lemon, toss into pasta or salads, or eat straight from the can with bread. The bones are soft and edible.
4. **Nutrition & your cycle (proposed).** Calcium needs are 1,000 mg a day for women aged 19 to 50 (NIH ODS); sardines eaten with their bones are one of the few non-dairy foods that give a substantial amount. We found no reliable research linking sardine to a particular phase of the cycle.
   *Currently:* Calcium needs are 1,000 mg a day for women aged 19 to 50 (NIH ODS); sardines eaten with their bones are one of the few non-dairy foods that give a substantial amount. No phase-specific claim is made.
5. **Good to know.** Fish allergen
   *Sources:* FDA, Food Allergies (the nine major food allergens; content current as of 03/11/2026; read 2026-10-28) (supports the existing caution)
